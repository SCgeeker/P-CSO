# Pinker Coherence — Calibration Examples

One before/after pair per principle — the hardest, most representative case. Use these to calibrate revisions; do not expect the input text to match them literally.

---

## Principle 1: Establish Topic and Point

BEFORE (buried lede):
> "Many factors influence comprehension. Prior research has examined various linguistic elements. Classifiers are morphemes that categorize nouns. Our study focuses on how they affect mental simulation."

AFTER (clear opening):
> "This study examines how Chinese classifiers affect mental simulation during comprehension. We test whether appropriate classifiers facilitate imagery activation compared to general classifiers."

RULE: Topic (Chinese classifiers) and point (their effect on mental simulation) must appear in the first 1–2 sentences, not be discovered on sentence 4.

---

## Principle 2: Maintain Information Flow

BEFORE:
> "The northern United States and Canada are places where herons live and breed. Spending the winter here has its advantages. Great Blue Herons live and breed in most of the northern United States. It's an advantage for herons to avoid the dangers of migration. Herons head south when the cold weather arrives."

AFTER (Mike O'Connor's original):
> "Great Blue Herons live and breed just about anywhere in the northern United States and most of Canada. When the cold weather arrives, the herons head south. A few come to Cape Cod where the winters usually aren't too bad."

RULE: The BEFORE version has a different grammatical subject in every sentence (United States / Spending / Great Blue Herons / It / Herons). The AFTER version keeps "herons" in subject position across nearly every sentence, and prepose temporal modifiers ("When the cold weather arrives") as given context before announcing new information in the main clause.

---

## Principle 3: Track Participants and Entities

BEFORE (synonymomania):
> "We used a sentence-picture verification task. The linguistic comprehension paradigm showed effects. This visual-verbal matching procedure revealed..."
- (Are these three different tasks?)

AFTER (consistent reference):
> "We used a sentence-picture verification task. The task showed effects. This task revealed..."
- (Clear: same task throughout)

RULE: First mention = indefinite article + full noun phrase; every later mention reuses the same term (or a pronoun), never a "stylistically elegant" synonym.

---

## Principle 4: Clarify Logical Relations

BEFORE (wrong connective — "also" signals similarity, not contrast):
> "Children's knowledge of how to use tools could be a result of experience, but also object affordances defined by shape and manipulability may provide cues such that humans do not require much time experimenting with an object."

AFTER:
> "Children's knowledge of how to use a tool could be a result of their experience with the tool; alternatively, it could be a result of their perceiving the tool's affordances from shape and manipulability cues."

RULE: "Also" signals similarity or elaboration — not contrast between two competing hypotheses. "Alternatively" names the actual logical relation. The AFTER version also holds both clauses in parallel ("it could be a result of…"), so the reader can see the contrast directly.

---

## Principle 5: Optimize Comparison and Contrast

BEFORE (non-parallel, three-way list):
> "Condition A increased accuracy, response times were reduced in Condition B, and for Condition C we observed no change."

AFTER (parallel, single variable):
> "Condition A increased accuracy, Condition B reduced response time, and Condition C showed no change."
- **Frame**: [Condition X] + [verb] + [outcome]; only the condition and its outcome vary.

RULE: Every item in a comparison set must share the identical syntactic frame. A three-way list is a harder test of this than a simple two-way contrast, since drift can creep in on any one of the parallel items.

---

## Principle 6: Handle Abstract Reference (Events and Situations)

BEFORE (nominalized on first mention — "zombie noun"):
> "The manipulation of classifier type in the sentence was conducted."

AFTER (verb first, nominalization second):
> "We manipulated classifier type in the sentence. This **manipulation** allowed us to test the amplification hypothesis."

RULE: First mention must be a verb showing the action; only later mentions may nominalize to act as a pronoun for that event.

---

## Principle 7: Check Author Attitude (Minimize Metadiscourse)

BEFORE (metadiscourse-heavy):
> "In this paper, I will argue that it is important to note that classifiers may have a role in mental simulation. As I will discuss below, I believe this is a crucial area for future research."

AFTER (direct):
> "Classifiers play a role in mental simulation. This area warrants further investigation."

RULE: Cut commentary about the text itself ("in this paper, I will argue...") and hedges not required by genuine uncertainty; let the ideas stand directly.

## Principle 8A: Agency in Subject Position (hidden person)

The hard case is telling a displaced agent from a legitimate abstraction. Both paragraphs
below come from the same section of one manuscript. One needs revision and one does not.

### Case A — displaced agents (revise)

**Before**

> Two recent reviews survey what cognitive scientists know about the memory functions of
> sleep. Both are organized around overnight retention. Both reviews read the recall
> advantage after a night of sleep as the signature of active consolidation.
> The reviews inherit both assumptions and argue for neither.

Subjects: *reviews*, *Both*, *Both reviews*, *The reviews* — four inanimate, zero animate.
Step 3 on each: a person can be named (Diekelmann; Rasch and Born), and naming them is truer,
because surveying, reading, inheriting and arguing are things authors do. All four are
displaced.

**After**

> Diekelmann and Rasch and Born review what cognitive scientists know about the memory
> functions of sleep. Both organize their reviews around overnight retention. Both read the
> recall advantage after a night of sleep as the signature of active consolidation. Neither
> argues for the assumptions they inherit.

The document survives as the object of *organize*, which is what a document can be.

### Case B — legitimate abstractions (leave alone)

> The overnight paradigm gives researchers little reason to consider sleep stages.
> Retention is scored on recall accuracy, and slow-wave duration is not an accuracy
> measure. A night's slow-wave duration is a value in the polysomnographic record, so no
> comparison of recall before and after sleep reaches that value. An overnight design
> therefore runs to completion without ever registering the difference. The paradigm is
> blind to the stages by construction rather than by oversight.

Five inanimate subjects, zero animate, and the paragraph is the best-written of its section.
Step 3 returns no on every one. The paragraph analyses what a paradigm can and cannot register,
so the paradigm belongs in subject position. Rewriting it as "Researchers score retention on
recall accuracy" would assert something the passage denies, namely that the blindness is a choice
researchers make rather than a property of the instrument.

**The lesson**: a low animate-subject ratio is not itself the defect. Case B scores 0/5 animate
and is correct. Run Step 3 on every inanimate subject before revising any of them.

## Principle 8B: Document as agent

Test A returns *no* on all of these, because naming a person would not make them truer.
Test B returns *yes*: a text is doing what only a claim can do.

**Before**

> Sleep does, and the sections after this one measure it.
> Two consequences follow for the sections in between.
> The sections that follow establish that the difference the criterion draws is consequential.

**After**

> Sleep does, and that gain is what the consolidation index measures.
> Two consequences follow. An index computed over nights from both sides of the
> slow-wave threshold averages across the two stages.
> The difference the criterion draws is consequential, and no accuracy measure recovers it.

The reader now learns what is true rather than where the writer filed it.

### The two keepers

Neither of these is a fault, and a run that removes them has over-applied 8B.

> Section \@ref(criterion) states the value that draws the boundary.

A cross-reference. The reader needs to know where the definition lives.

> A formal criterion has no claim on a cognitive scientist's attention until the cost of
> working without it is visible.

A reason for an ordering decision. Compare the version that fails, "That criterion is the
subject of the next section", which announces the order without justifying it.
